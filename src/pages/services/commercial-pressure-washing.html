Commercial pressure washing in Houston is about more than curb appeal. A gray, gum-spotted entry tells customers something before they reach the door, a greasy dumpster pad draws flies and inspectors, and a slick walkway is a hazard. The right company cleans all of it and also knows where the dirty water is allowed to go.

What is commercial pressure washing?

It is exterior cleaning for business property: the concrete, masonry, glass storefronts, metal canopies and pavement that customers and staff use every day. Three things set it apart from washing a house.

  • Size and access. A strip center sidewalk or a parking deck covers far more ground than a driveway, and the work often has to fit around open hours and parked cars.
  • What is on the surface. Restaurants, lube shops and car washes deal with cooking grease, motor oil and food waste, not just mildew and pollen. Grease needs the right detergent and often hot water, not just pressure.
  • Where the water goes. On a commercial site, wash water usually has to be contained and recovered rather than left to run down the curb. In Houston, grease work also brings a city permit into the picture.

Commercial properties and what each one usually needs

  • Retail and strip centers Entry walks with gum and spilled drinks, storefront glass frames, columns, and the dark band of grime where the sidewalk meets the building.
  • Restaurants and drive-thrus Grease around back doors and the dumpster enclosure, food spills in the drive-thru lane, and stained concrete under the menu board.
  • Offices and medical buildings Building washing for stucco, brick or tilt-wall panels, plus entrances and ramps that patients and visitors use daily.
  • Apartment and condo common areas Breezeways, stairs, pool decks, mail kiosks and the trash corral, often scheduled building by building.
  • Parking lots and garages Oil drips in parking stalls, tire marks, and the stained ramps and stairwells of a parking deck.
  • Warehouses and loading docks Dock aprons, truck bays and the tilt-wall face that faces the street, where diesel soot and dust settle.

Houston's rules on grease and wash water

The storm sewer is for rain. Chapter 47 of the City of Houston Code of Ordinances makes it an offense to put any discharge into the city's storm sewer system that is not made up entirely of storm water. Grease, detergent and grime rinsed off a parking lot fall outside that.

Grease areas need a permitted washer. The Houston Health Department's Special Waste program tracks fats, oils and grease. Its list of special waste generators includes restaurants, car washes, lube stations, laundromats, dry cleaners and any site with a grease trap or interceptor. Its guidance says those businesses may only use a pressure washing company holding a current Health Department pressure washer permit and equipped to collect all of its wash water.

To get that permit, the application says all of a company's equipment (booms, mats and recovery systems) must be tested by the City of Houston to make sure wastewater is recovered and storm drains are protected. It also asks for proof of financial responsibility for each vehicle and trailer and for the company name and phone number on both sides of the vehicle.

In the City of Houston, companies that wash restaurant and commercial grease areas need a Houston Health Department pressure washer permit. Ask the company you are matched with whether it holds one, and ask to see it if your property has a grease trap or a dumpster pad.

Health inspectors look at more than the kitchen. Department guidance says dumpsters and compactors must sit on a smooth, nonabsorbent surface such as concrete or machine-laid asphalt, and inspectors check the dumpster, rendering bin and the area around them. A clean pad is part of passing that look.

Outside city limits the details change, but the idea does not. In unincorporated Harris County, around places like Spring, county stormwater rules allow routine pavement washing only without detergents, other chemicals or heat. Sugar Land's stormwater ordinance lists water containing detergents or chemicals as a prohibited discharge, and Missouri City runs a hotline for reporting illicit discharges. A good commercial company plans containment for the city it is working in.

How a careful commercial crew handles runoff

You do not need to supervise the equipment. These are the things worth watching for, drawn from EPA guidance on outdoor washing and from state agency guidance on mobile power washing.

  1. Drains are blocked or covered first. Storm inlets near the work area get covered with mats or plugs before any water hits the ground.
  2. The work area is bermed. Rubber berms, water-filled tubes or sandbags hold wash water where it can be picked up.
  3. Water is vacuumed up, not swept to the curb. A recovery system pulls the water into a tank on the truck or trailer.
  4. Collected water goes somewhere approved. That means a sanitary sewer connection the treatment plant has allowed, or a licensed hauler. Houston Public Works handles requests to discharge washdown water to the sanitary sewer.
  5. Grease is cleaned, not moved. Hot water and the right degreaser break oil down so it can be recovered, instead of pushing it across the lot.

Safety, slips and first impressions

Shaded entries, ramps and pool decks around Houston grow a film of algae in the humid months, and drive-thru lanes collect grease that gets slick in rain. OSHA's rule on walking and working surfaces (29 CFR 1910.22) requires employers to keep those surfaces clean and free of hazards such as spills and leaks. Washing is not a substitute for a safety program, but a slick back-door walk or loading ramp is a good reason to put cleaning on the calendar.

Appearance matters too. Gum, drip lines under the awning and a green north wall read as neglect, even when the inside is spotless.

Planning around your business hours

Most businesses do not want wet concrete, hoses and equipment between customers and the front door. Many commercial properties schedule washing early in the morning, in the evening or on a closed day. Near homes, the City of Houston's noise rules also come into play, so not every time slot will suit every site. Ask about timing when you call, and confirm the agreed window with the company in writing.

A few things make scheduling smoother:

  • Tell the company which areas must stay open, such as one entrance or the drive-thru lane.
  • Say where the nearest water spigot is and whether the company may use it.
  • Mention any grease trap, dumpster enclosure or parking deck, since those change the equipment needed.
