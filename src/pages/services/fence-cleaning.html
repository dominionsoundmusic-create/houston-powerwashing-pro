Fence cleaning in Houston usually starts with a fence that has quietly changed color. New cedar or pine goes silver within a few summers, then the bottom boards near the sprinklers turn green, then black specks of mildew show up on the shady side. A good fence cleaning company can bring most of that back without leaving the boards fuzzy or striped.

Three kinds of fence, three different jobs

Wood privacy fences

The most common fence behind Houston homes, and the one that needs the most care. Cedar and pressure-treated pine both gray in the sun and grow mildew where they stay damp. The goal is to lift the gray layer and the growth with a cleaning solution and gentle rinsing, not to strip wood away.

Vinyl fences

Vinyl does not gray, but it does turn green on the shaded face, collect sprinkler spots and pick up red clay splashed up by storms. It cleans with a soft wash and rinse. Too much pressure can scuff the finish and leave a dull patch that looks worse than the dirt.

Wrought iron and metal

Iron fences and pool gates collect grime, mildew on the shaded side and rust where paint has chipped. Washing removes dirt and loose flakes before repainting. Heavy pressure can peel sound paint and open more spots to rust, so lower pressure and hand detail work better.

Why Houston wood fences go gray and green

The gray is weathering. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory explains that ultraviolet light in sunlight breaks down the outer surface of wood, rain washes away the broken-down material, and the fibers left behind give the wood a silvery-gray sheen. Repeated wetting and drying roughens the surface and raises the grain.

The green and black come from Houston's damp air. Bush Intercontinental averages about 52 inches of rain a year under the National Weather Service's current normals, and Purdue Extension notes that mildew grows most in warm, humid climates on surfaces where shrubs and trees block air movement. That describes the back fence line in most Houston subdivisions: shrubs on one side, a neighbor's tree overhead, and a sprinkler head soaking the bottom rail.

Does power washing damage wood?

This is the question most people ask before they hire anyone, and the honest answer is that it depends on how it is done. Wood has soft, light bands of growth between harder, darker ones. A tight, powerful spray carves into the soft bands first. The result is called furring: a raised, hairy, splintery surface that looks like the wood is growing fuzz. Hold the spray in one spot or swing it unevenly and you also get lap marks and stripes across the boards.

Furring is not just cosmetic. Stain soaks into the torn fibers unevenly, so a damaged fence has to be sanded before it will take stain well. On a 150-foot backyard fence, that is a lot of sanding.

The people who make stains and treated lumber say the same thing. Behr's fence guidance says that if a pressure washer is used to rinse, it should be on its broadest spray pattern and held well back from the wood. YellaWood, a maker of pressure-treated pine, says mild soap and low-pressure washing with a wide fan, keeping the wand moving, cleans surface mold and dirt without damaging the wood. A careful fence cleaning company works that way and lets the cleaning solution, not the pressure, do the heavy lifting.

Cleaning before stain or paint

Many fence calls are really the first step of a staining project. Stain needs bare, clean, dry wood to soak into. Here is a sensible order of events, so you can line up the cleaning and the staining without wasting a weekend.

  1. Cleaning day. The company treats the gray and mildew, rinses gently and keeps runoff and cleaner off your plants and lawn.
  2. Drying time. Sherwin-Williams suggests about 24 to 48 hours after cleaning, and Behr says to let the wood dry completely. In Houston humidity, or on a fence in deep shade, plan for the longer end and check before you start.
  3. Repairs. Loose pickets, popped nails and a leaning post are easier to spot on a clean fence. Fix them before stain goes on.
  4. Stain or seal. Staining is a separate job. Some fence cleaning companies also stain, others do not, so ask when you call.

The Forest Products Laboratory points out that refinishing reduces the cracking, raised grain and mildew that unmaintained outdoor wood develops. A fence that is cleaned and protected tends to stay presentable longer than one that is only washed.

HOA letters and city rules about fences

A stained or mildewed fence is one of the things deed restriction committees notice. A few examples from around the Houston area:

  • Sugar Land: the First Colony Community Services Association lists mildew, mold and stains on the home, fence or driveway among its ten most often cited deed restriction violations. Its process uses three letters, each with a 30-day cure period.
  • Missouri City: the city's code enforcement page lists mildew among common concerns and says fences must be kept structurally sound and in good repair.
  • The Woodlands: exterior changes to a lot with an existing home, including fences, need prior written approval from the village Residential Design Review Committee. If you plan to stain a freshly cleaned fence a new color, check with your RDRC first.

If you have a letter with a deadline, mention it when you call so the company knows the timing matters to you. It cannot promise a date until it sees the job, but it can tell you its next opening.

What to tell the line about your fence

The automated assistant asks what needs cleaning and how big it is. For a fence, these details help the company give you a realistic answer faster:

  • Material: cedar, pine, vinyl, iron, or a mix.
  • Roughly how many feet, and whether both sides need cleaning. The neighbor's side usually needs their permission.
  • Height, and whether there are shrubs, vines or a flower bed along the base.
  • Whether you plan to stain or paint afterward, and when.
  • Any HOA notice and its deadline.
