Why pressure alone fails on oil

Oil does not sit on top of concrete. It wicks down into the pores, and the deeper it goes, the less a spray can reach it. Blasting the spot cleans the top layer, so the stain looks better for a while. Then oil still in the slab works back toward the surface and the mark reappears. Repeated hard blasting can also rough up the surface, which leaves it more open to the next spill.

Concrete Network lists the tools that do reach deeper oil: strong detergents, concrete degreasers, poultices that draw oil up as they dry, and microbial cleaners that break the oil down. It also points out the limit. Typical degreasers loosen oil but do not break it down, which is why an old, heavy stain may end up lighter rather than gone.

Rust stain removal on concrete

Rust stains are iron that has oxidized on the surface, so they ignore degreasers and need an acid-based rust remover made for concrete. Concrete Network describes oxalic acid as the classic choice for this. Acids can also change the color or texture of the surface if they are misused, which is why a careful company tests a small, out-of-the-way spot first and rinses thoroughly afterward.

Around Houston, the orange fans along the edge of a driveway or walk usually trace back to sprinklers. Concrete Network names irrigation water with high iron content as a common cause, especially from wells. Many homes on acreage outside towns like Tomball water from private wells, so if your stains line up with sprinkler heads, the well is the first thing to ask about. Iron-containing fertilizer is the other common source.

What to expect from stain work

Honest companies set expectations before they start. Ask for one of these three answers for each stain, and be wary of anyone who promises the first one for everything without looking.

  • "It should come out." Fresh oil, most fertilizer rust and light sprinkler fans.
  • "It should get much lighter." Oil that has sat for months, deep rust fans that have built up over several summers.
  • "Some shadow may stay." Years-old oil patches, transmission fluid or coolant that has dyed the concrete, and stains on unsealed slabs.

Oil and wash water do not belong in the street drain

Degreaser, loosened oil and rust remover should not run into the gutter at the curb. The City of Houston's storm water ordinance makes it an offense to send anything other than storm water into the city's storm sewer system, and Houston Public Works lists anything that forms a floating layer of oil and grease among prohibited sewer discharges. EPA guidance on washing calls for containing wash water rather than letting it reach storm drains.

In practice, that means a careful company blocks or covers the nearest inlet, soaks up oily residue instead of hosing it away, and keeps cleaning products on the driveway rather than flowing down the street. Ask how the company handles runoff when you get the quote.

Keep the next stain from setting in

  • Fix the leak. If the same parking spot stains every few months, the car is the problem. A drip pan buys time.
  • Blot fresh spills quickly. An absorbent on a fresh drip keeps oil from sinking in. Do not hose it, which only spreads it.
  • Re-aim the sprinklers. Heads that spray onto the slab both waste water and paint the concrete orange.
  • Sweep fertilizer off the concrete. Do it before the sprinklers run or the next rain.
  • Consider a sealer. Sealed concrete gives you time to wipe up a spill before it soaks in. It must go on fully dry concrete.

Before you call

Take a quick photo of each stain and count them. Note whether the driveway has ever been sealed, roughly how long each stain has been there, and whether you water from a well. Stain work is usually priced along with a full driveway cleaning, since a treated patch can look patchy beside an unwashed slab.
