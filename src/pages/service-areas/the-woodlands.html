Why pressure washing in The Woodlands starts with the trees

The Woodlands was planned around its forest. George Mitchell's community opened in October 1974 with the idea that houses would sit among the trees instead of replacing them, and pressure washing in The Woodlands today is mostly about living with what that canopy drops and the shade it casts.

The Township's forestry plan describes loblolly and slash pine with red and water oak overhead, magnolia, dogwood and crepe myrtle in the middle, and yaupon and wax myrtle underneath. On a typical lot that means a north wall that rarely dries, black streaks working down the shingles, needles packed into gutters and roof valleys, and a driveway that goes dark in a band where the branches hang over it. Every spring, oak pollen lays a yellow film on decks, sills and patio furniture.

The local rules that shape a cleaning job

The Woodlands is a township, not a city. About 114,000 people (114,436 at the 2020 census) live across nine villages, from Grogan's Mill and Panther Creek to Sterling Ridge and Creekside Park, mostly in Montgomery County with a southern piece in Harris County. Exterior standards here come from covenants rather than a city code, and that changes who you ask about what.

  • Covenants, not ordinancesCommunity standards are enforced through deed covenants, which The Woodlands Township has administered since 2010.
  • A design committee in each villageEvery village elects a Residential Design Review Committee of resident volunteers that meets monthly.
  • Approval before a new lookPrior written RDRC approval is needed for exterior changes such as a new paint color, a deck, a fence or paving.
  • Two sprinkler nights a weekWoodlands Water's permanent schedule limits automatic irrigation to two set nights, 8 p.m. to 6 a.m.
  • Storm drains feed waterwaysWoodlands Water asks residents to keep chemicals, debris and waste out of storm drains, which lead to local creeks.
  • Concerns go through 311Neighbors can flag a possible covenant violation through the Township's 311 Report a Concern portal.

For a cleaning job, the covenants matter most at the finishing stage. Washing a fence, deck or driveway is upkeep. Putting a different stain color on that fence, or a tinted sealer on those pavers, is the kind of change the RDRC reviews. The Township's residential standards go into detail on fence finishes and colors, so read the current version or call Covenant Administration before you pick a product. A good company will ask what you plan to put on the wood once it dries instead of assuming.

Three surfaces the forest is hardest on

Roofs and gutters

Needles collect in valleys, behind chimneys and along gutter runs, holding moisture against the roof. The dark streaks on shingles are mostly an algae called Gloeocapsa magma. GAF, Owens Corning and the Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association all warn against high-pressure washing because it strips the protective granules. A careful company soft washes and rinses gently. See roof cleaning and gutter cleaning.

Shaded siding and brick

Purdue Extension notes that mildew is most common on walls behind trees and shrubs, where air barely moves. In The Woodlands that describes a lot of north and east walls. Fiber cement, vinyl and painted trim should be soft washed at low pressure, because a hard spray can force water behind the boards. See house washing and soft washing.

Wood fences and decks

The USDA Forest Products Laboratory explains that sunlight turns bare wood a silvery gray, while repeated wetting and drying raises the grain. Under pines, gray also picks up mildew. Too much pressure tears the soft wood fibers and leaves a fuzzy surface, so a gentle method matters. See fence cleaning.

Plan the wash around sprinkler nights

If Woodlands Water serves your home in the Montgomery County part of the community, automatic sprinklers follow a fixed two-night schedule. Booking a house or fence wash for the morning after a dry night means the surface is not already soaked, and the sprinklers will not spray fresh dirt onto clean brick that evening.

Hand watering with a nozzle and drip irrigation are exempt from the schedule. If you live in the Harris County part of the community or get water from another provider, check that provider's rules instead.

While you are thinking about sprinklers, look at where the heads point. A head that wets the bottom row of brick or the same fence panels twice a week keeps that strip green no matter how often it is cleaned. Turning it a few degrees is the cheapest maintenance there is.

Rinse water and the creeks that run through the villages

Storm drains in The Woodlands carry runoff straight into the creeks and drainage channels that wind between the villages, with no treatment on the way. That is why Woodlands Water asks residents to keep chemicals out of them. Spring Creek and Panther Branch are not abstract here either: Hurricane Harvey in 2017 flooded homes along both, including more than 300 in the Timarron area.

A careful company keeps soft wash solution and grime out of the street inlet by blocking it, steering the rinse toward the lawn where that is appropriate, and never washing grease or cleaning product into the gutter. If your fence or slab still carries a dark silt line from past high water, mention it on the call so the company knows what it is looking at.
