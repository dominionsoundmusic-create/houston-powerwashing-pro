Pressure washing in Sugar Land, a sugar town turned suburb

Sugar Land is named for what it used to grow and refine. In 1843 Matthew Williams set up a commercial sugar mill on Oyster Creek, on the site that became Imperial Sugar. The city incorporated in 1959, and from 1976 First Colony began turning former cane fields into neighborhoods. Pressure washing in Sugar Land now mostly happens in those master-planned communities, where associations keep a close eye on mildew.

About 111,000 people (111,026 at the 2020 census) live in northeastern Fort Bend County between Oyster Creek, US 90A and the Brazos River. Many homes back onto lakes or the creek, and several neighborhoods sit inside levee improvement districts along the Brazos. Water nearby and mature shade trees overhead keep north walls, fence boards and pool decks damp, which is exactly where green film and black spots take hold.

How First Colony's deed restriction process runs

The First Colony Community Services Association lists "mildew/mold/stains" on the home, the fence or the driveway and walkway among its Top Ten most often cited deed restriction violations. Its enforcement follows three written steps, each with a 30-day cure period.

  1. Courtesy notice. The friendliest letter and the best moment to act. Thirty days is enough time to get a company out, as long as you call early rather than in the last week.
  2. Violation notice. If the surface is still dirty when the association checks again, a formal notice follows with another 30 days to cure.
  3. Certified letter. The last notice before fines. Under the policy that took effect September 1, 2023, fines rose from $200 to $400 per month, and a fine can be waived only once in any 12 months.

Once the work is done, keep the dated invoice and take a few photos of the cleaned surfaces. If the association re-inspects, that record answers the question quickly. Telfair, Greatwood, New Territory and the other communities have their own deed restrictions and processes, so check your association's current rules rather than assuming they match First Colony's.

What needs washing depends on where you live in Sugar Land

Established First Colony streets

The oldest homes here went up in the years after development began in 1976. Trees have had decades to grow, so shade, leaf staining and mildew on brick and fences are common. Long-parked cars leave oil on garage aprons. A house wash plus oil and rust stain treatment often covers it.

Lake and creek lots

Backyards on a lake or along Oyster Creek stay humid. Decks, patios and the lake side of the fence go green first. Runoff matters most here, because whatever goes down the storm drain heads for that water. See deck and patio cleaning.

Telfair, Greatwood and New Territory

Telfair land joined the city in July 2004, and Greatwood and New Territory were annexed in December 2017. Homeowners there now follow the City's stormwater and drought rules as well as their own association's standards, which is worth knowing before a summer driveway or fence cleaning.

Inside the levees after a big rain

Levees keep the Brazos River out, but they cannot stop rain from falling inside them. During Hurricane Harvey in 2017 the river crested above 55 feet and no river water entered Fort Bend County Levee Improvement District No. 2, which protects about 5,000 acres of First Colony. Even so, more than 31 inches of rain fell inside that levee and over 200 homes flooded from rainfall alone.

Heavy storms leave silt on driveways, splash lines on brick and debris against fences. Once yards have drained, a cleanup is a reasonable thing to book. Tell the company what the water reached so it can judge which surfaces need what.
