When a deed-restriction letter shows up

Associations near Pearland run regular inspections. Silverlake's management company, for example, drives the neighborhood for deed-restriction issues once a month. Here is how the process usually unfolds, and where the free line fits.

  1. The inspection. Someone notes mildew on a wall, a dark driveway or a stained fence from the street.
  2. The letter. You get notice with a date to fix it. Silverlake's published FAQ says nothing further happens if the problem is fixed by that date. State law points the same way: section 209.006 of the Texas Property Code generally requires a certified-mail notice and a reasonable chance to cure before any fine.
  3. The call. Read the letter to the assistant if it helps. "Mildew on the stucco above the garage and the front walk" tells the company exactly what to look at.
  4. The quote and the work. The company you are connected with inspects, prices and schedules the job with you. Keep its dated invoice.
  5. The follow-up. Ask your association how it wants you to report the fix. Some owners send photos with the invoice.

Stucco, brick and the Shadow Creek Ranch rules

Shadow Creek Ranch is a good example of how specific these rules get. Pearland annexed the land between 1996 and 1998, home sales began in 2001, and the first phase came with four lakes. The association's newsletter has told residents plainly that mildew and a lack of upkeep are against the deed restrictions. Its recorded Architectural Review Committee guidelines go further: paint on stucco must include a mildew retardant, repainting a house or fence needs an application first, and owners are responsible for maintaining the driveways serving their lots.

Stucco is the surface to be careful with. Its rough texture holds mildew, and the same porousness means high pressure can crack it, chip it or drive water behind the wall. A company that plans to soft wash stucco and pressure wash only the flatwork is the one you want.

Clear Creek, Mary's Creek and the storm drain out front

Pearland knows high water. During Hurricane Harvey the Mary's Creek gauge in Pearland recorded 49.2 inches of rain, at the time a Texas record for a tropical system, and more than 1,700 homes and over 50 businesses in the city took on structural flooding, mostly along Clear Creek. Clear Creek is also on the state's list of waters impaired for bacteria.

That is why where the rinse water goes is a fair question. Chapter 30 of Pearland's Code of Ordinances contains an article titled "Illicit Discharge Prohibitions." Grime, mildew and cleaning solution flushed into the gutter head toward local creeks. Ask the company you are connected with how it handles runoff near the curb inlet, and whether it protects your beds and lawn before any solution goes on.

Watering days, dry summers and timing a wash

Pearland draws on two sources. Ten city wells pump from the Evangeline and Chicot aquifers, and surface water comes through agreements with the City of Houston and the Gulf Coast Water Authority. The city's new surface water treatment plant started pumping in late 2024, treating about 10 million gallons a day from the authority's American Canal.

In a drought, the city's plan starts with voluntary limits in Stage 1: outdoor watering twice a week on your trash day, and none between 9 a.m. and 7 p.m. Stage 2 makes those limits mandatory. The city also discourages runoff that runs 50 feet or more down the street or pools more than a quarter inch deep. If a dry summer has the city in a drought stage, raise it when the company calls you back so the job can be planned around it.
