How Kingwood's trees change the job

Kingwood is a master-planned community of about 14,000 acres, started in 1970 by King Ranch and Friendswood Development Company, an Exxon subsidiary. The name joins the two. The developer coined the "Livable Forest" slogan for its trees, and the community kept them: there are more than 75 miles of greenbelt trails, and the Kingwood Service Association still looks after greenbelts and parks such as River Grove, Deer Ridge and East End Park.

For a house, that canopy means shade on the roof, on the siding and on the driveway for most of the day. Surfaces that never bake dry grow mildew, algae and lichen. The right answer is usually a gentler method rather than more pressure, because the growth has to be killed, not just sprayed off, or it is back by next season.

Two layers of rules: your village and the City of Houston

Your village association

Kingwood is divided into villages, and each has a community association that enforces its own deed restrictions. Changes such as new siding, new paint or a roof replacement need written approval from the Architectural Control Committee before work begins, submitted through the association's management company.

Associations also inspect. Kingwood Place West, for one, says its deed restriction inspections are typically monthly, and violation letters set a deadline to fix the problem.

The City of Houston

Because Houston has no zoning, the city's Legal Department runs a deed restriction enforcement team that can take complaints, send violation letters and go to court. As part of the city since 1996, Kingwood falls under it.

The city's storm water ordinance also applies here. Chapter 47 of the Code of Ordinances makes it an offense to put "any discharge that is not composed entirely of storm water" into the storm sewer.

Why runoff matters so much in Kingwood

Storm drains here run toward the San Jacinto River and Lake Houston, and Lake Houston is one of the City of Houston's main drinking-water sources. A careful company covers or blocks the nearest inlets, steers rinse water onto the lawn when it is safe to, and never pushes cleaning solution, oil or dirty wash water into the gutter. On restaurant or grease-heavy business sites, ask whether the company holds the Houston Health Department's pressure washer permit, which requires the city to test its wastewater recovery equipment.
