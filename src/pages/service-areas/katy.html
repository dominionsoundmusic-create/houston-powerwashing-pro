What pressure washing in Katy is up against

Katy grew out of flat coastal prairie. It started as Cane Island, opened its post office in 1896, and turned to rice after 1901. Texas's first concrete rice dryers went up here in 1944. Most of what people now call Katy came much later, as master-planned neighborhoods spread across the old fields, so pressure washing in Katy usually means wide driveways, brick-and-siding two-story homes and long runs of shared wood fence.

Open country brings wind, and wind brings dust. Soil splashes onto the lowest course of brick and the bottom siding boards every time a storm blows through, and the side of a fence that never sees sun turns green. In late winter and early spring, oak pollen settles on patios and cars. Summer flips the problem around: heat drives up water demand, and that can put limits on washing hard surfaces.

Drought stages and a summer wash in Katy

The City of Katy's drought contingency plan names power washing directly. As conditions worsen, it asks residents to cut water used for power washing buildings, sidewalks, driveways, parking lots and other hard surfaces, and its stricter stages go further and restrict hosing down paved areas and buildings. The exact wording for each stage is in the City's code, so read the current version rather than relying on memory.

This is not theoretical. The City called Stage 2 and Stage 3 restrictions in recent summers, including 2022 and 2023, when heat pushed water demand up.

  1. Find your current stage. Check your water provider's website or bill insert. Inside city limits that is the City of Katy; elsewhere it is your district.
  2. Mention it on the call. Tell the company you are put through to which stage is in effect. It can say whether the job fits the rules now or should wait.
  3. Ask where the rinse water goes. Katy's Public Works department runs a stormwater program that takes reports of illicit discharges. Soapy water belongs on the lawn or in containment, not in the street inlet.
  4. Move the job if you can. If the cleaning is not urgent, a spring or fall date avoids planning around a summer stage at all.

Barker Reservoir and old flood lines

Some Katy neighborhoods carry a particular history. During Hurricane Harvey in 2017, water pooled behind Barker Reservoir and entered thousands of homes in parts of Cinco Ranch, Kelliwood and Canyon Gate. The Addicks and Barker dams were built in the 1940s to protect Buffalo Bayou, and in 2019 a federal judge found the government liable for flooding upstream of them.

Years later, a tide line can still show on a back fence, a garden wall or the lower brick of a garage. If you have one, describe it on the call. A company can look at whether it is a surface stain that cleaning can lighten or a mark that has gone into the material, and tell you honestly before you pay for anything.

Big associations and shared fences

Katy's large master-planned communities lean on deed restrictions to keep streets looking uniform. Cinco Ranch, for example, began in 1984 when a group of developers bought the ranch for a 5,000-acre planned community, and associations there enforce deed restrictions. Some associations also maintain perimeter and common-area fencing, including cutting back ivy and other growth. Before you hire anyone to clean a fence, confirm whether it is yours or the association's, and check your association's current rules on stain colors.
